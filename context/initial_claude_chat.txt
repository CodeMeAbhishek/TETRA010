HealthTech Problem Statement 1: AI-Powered Lifestyle Disease Risk Prediction & Early Referral Assistant
What they're actually asking for

Strip away the language and this is a clinical decision support tool that does four things in sequence:

    Takes in patient data (demographics, symptoms, vitals, history, meds, labs) — likely incomplete, since that's called out as a real-world constraint
    Scores risk across 5 conditions: diabetes, hypertension, CKD, cardiovascular disease, stroke
    Finds gaps — tells the clinician what tests are missing and should be ordered
    Recommends referral when risk crosses a threshold, and explains why (explainability is explicitly required)

The non-negotiable constraints baked into the statement: works offline of any hospital system, multilingual, affordable, explainable, deployable in low-resource primary care. Judges will check for these specifically — they're not throwaway lines.
Why this problem is winnable (and where teams lose)

Most teams will build "patient enters symptoms → LLM says risk: high/medium/low." That's weak because:

    It's not explainable (black box LLM output ≠ clinical reasoning)
    It ignores the "missing investigations" requirement entirely
    It has no grounding in real clinical guidelines, so a judge with any medical background will poke holes in 30 seconds

Winning approach

1. Use real, validated risk models as the backbone — not a raw LLM guess

This is the single biggest differentiator. Don't ask an LLM "does this patient have diabetes risk" — compute it with known clinical scores, then use the LLM to interpret/explain/personalize:

    Diabetes → ADA risk score / Indian Diabetes Risk Score (IDRS) — well suited since it's calibrated for Indian populations (waist circumference, physical activity, family history, age)
    Hypertension → JNC8 / ACC-AHA BP categories + risk factor count
    CVD → ASCVD risk calculator (Pooled Cohort Equations) or WHO CVD risk charts (has a version calibrated for South Asia — big credibility point)
    CKD → KDIGO risk categories using eGFR + albuminuria (or estimate risk factors when labs unavailable)
    Stroke → CHA₂DS₂-VASc-style or simple validated stroke risk indicators

This gives you two huge wins simultaneously: explainability is free (you can literally show "your risk score is 18/26 because of X, Y, Z factors — per IDRS guidelines"), and you're defensible under judge scrutiny because you're citing real medicine, not hallucinated thresholds.

2. Layer the LLM on top, not underneath

Use the LLM for:

    Filling the gap when data is incomplete ("no lipid panel available — flag as missing investigation, recommend ordering it")
    Turning the score into plain-language, multilingual patient education
    Drafting the referral note/summary for the clinician

This is architecturally the right story to tell judges: "We don't let the LLM invent a diagnosis — it explains and communicates around a clinically validated scoring engine." That sentence alone signals maturity.

3. Handle missing data explicitly — this is a stated requirement, don't skip it

Build a simple rules layer: if a required input for a given score is missing, the system (a) still computes a partial/conservative risk estimate using what's available, and (b) outputs "Recommended test: HbA1c not available — recommend ordering" tied to that specific score. This directly answers "identify missing investigations" from the brief — most teams will miss this.

4. Keep the UI dead simple and demo-able in under 3 minutes

    Single form or voice/simple chat input for vitals + symptoms + history
    One dashboard screen: 5 risk gauges (diabetes/HTN/CKD/CVD/stroke), color-coded
    "Missing tests" panel
    "Why this score" expandable panel (explainability)
    Referral banner if any score crosses threshold, with one-tap "generate referral note"
    Toggle for regional language (even 2 languages — English + Hindi/Gujarati — is enough to prove the point)

5. Address "works independently of hospital IT systems"

Just make it a standalone web app with local/offline-capable data entry (this also sets up nicely if you want to reuse code from the AgriTech offline theme, but not necessary). Say explicitly in your pitch: "No EHR integration needed — a health worker with a phone can use this in a PHC."
Suggested tech stack (fast to build in a hackathon window)

    Frontend: simple React/HTML form + dashboard (Chart.js/Recharts for gauges)
    Risk engine: Python (rule-based/formula implementation of IDRS, ASCVD, KDIGO, etc.) exposed via a small API
    LLM layer: Claude API for explanation generation, missing-data reasoning, multilingual patient summaries, referral note drafting
    Data: no need for a real EHR — use a synthetic patient dataset (5–10 sample profiles) to demo range of risk levels

Pitch narrative for judges

Open with the stat: preventable complications in rural India often trace back to late detection, not lack of treatment options. Then position your tool as: "Not another chatbot — a clinically-grounded second opinion that a primary care worker with minimal training can run in under 2 minutes, in their own language, on a phone." Close with the explainability + missing-investigation angle since that's what separates a "cool AI demo" from an actual clinical decision support tool.

IDRS/ASCVD/CKD first of all I need to understand this things

Let's go through each one — what it measures, what inputs it needs, and how the score works.
1. IDRS (Indian Diabetes Risk Score)

Built specifically for the Indian population (unlike Western scores which don't account for lower BMI thresholds and higher genetic predisposition in Indians). It's simple — only 4 parameters, all easy to collect without a lab test.
Parameter	Category	Points
Age	<35 yrs	0
	35–49 yrs	20
	≥50 yrs	30
Waist circumference	Male <90cm / Female <80cm	0
	Male 90–99cm / Female 80–89cm	10
	Male ≥100cm / Female ≥90cm	20
Physical activity	Vigorous exercise	0
	Moderate exercise	10
	Mild exercise	20
	No exercise	30
Family history of diabetes	No family history	0
	One parent diabetic	10
	Both parents diabetic	20

Total score interpretation:

    <30 → Low risk
    30–50 → Moderate risk
    ≥60 → High risk (this population has ~50% chance of having/developing diabetes)

Why it's great for your project: zero lab dependency, so it works even with the "incomplete records" constraint mentioned in the problem statement, and it's literally designed for the demographic your solution targets.
2. ASCVD Risk Score (Pooled Cohort Equations)

Estimates 10-year risk of a first heart attack or stroke (atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease). This one is more lab-dependent and mathematically heavier — it's not a simple point table like IDRS, it's a logistic regression equation.

Inputs required:

    Age (40–79 yrs range is what it's validated for)
    Sex
    Race (has separate coefficients for African American vs. White/Other — this is actually a known limitation for using it directly on Indian populations, which is why WHO has a South Asia-calibrated version you should consider instead)
    Total cholesterol
    HDL cholesterol
    Systolic blood pressure
    Whether on BP treatment or not
    Smoking status (yes/no)
    Diabetes status (yes/no)

How it works: each variable has a coefficient, they get summed in a weighted equation, then converted into a 10-year percentage risk (e.g., "12% risk of a cardiovascular event in 10 years").

Risk categories:

    <5% → Low
    5–7.5% → Borderline
    7.5–20% → Intermediate
    ≥20% → High

Practical note for your build: since you need cholesterol values (lab-dependent), this is exactly where your "missing investigation" logic kicks in — if lipid panel isn't available, the system should flag "Recommend lipid panel to compute CVD risk" rather than guessing.

I'd suggest using the WHO CVD Risk Chart for South Asia as your actual formula — it's simpler (uses age, sex, smoking, BP, and either cholesterol OR just BMI as a substitute when labs aren't available), which fits your "resource-limited setting" requirement much better than the US-based ASCVD equation.
3. CKD Risk (KDIGO framework)

CKD staging isn't a single "score" like the others — it's a 2D grid combining two lab values:

Step 1 — eGFR (estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate) tells you kidney function level:
GFR Category	eGFR (mL/min/1.73m²)	Description
G1	≥90	Normal
G2	60–89	Mildly decreased
G3a	45–59	Mild–moderate decrease
G3b	30–44	Moderate–severe decrease
G4	15–29	Severe decrease
G5	<15	Kidney failure

Step 2 — Albuminuria (ACR — urine Albumin-Creatinine Ratio) tells you kidney damage level:
Category	ACR (mg/g)	Description
A1	<30	Normal/mild
A2	30–300	Moderate
A3	>300	Severe

Step 3 — combine them into KDIGO's color-coded heat map (Green/Yellow/Orange/Red = risk of progression, kidney failure, cardiovascular events, and death). E.g., G1+A1 = Green (low risk); G4+A3 = Red (very high risk).

For your prototype: eGFR needs serum creatinine + age + sex (calculated via the CKD-EPI formula), and ACR needs a urine test — both are labs that a rural PHC may not have readily. This is your strongest "missing investigation" showcase: if creatinine is available, compute eGFR directly; if not, fall back to risk-factor-based flagging (diabetes + hypertension + age + family history = "high pre-test probability for CKD, recommend eGFR + ACR testing").
How these three fit together in your architecture

    IDRS → pure risk-factor based, no labs needed, always computable
    WHO CVD chart → mostly risk-factor based, labs optional
    CKD/KDIGO → lab-dependent, best showcase for "missing investigation" logic

This gradient is actually a good story for judges: your system doesn't just output one type of number, it demonstrates graceful degradation — the more data available, the more precise the score, and it always tells the clinician exactly what to collect next to get there.
